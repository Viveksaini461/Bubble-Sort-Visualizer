Supplements 101: What to Take, What to Skip, and How to Buy Smart

By Maya Chen, RD, MS · Registered Dietitian & Sports Nutritionist · July 5, 2025 · 12 min read

Supplements can be useful tools, but they are not magic. Think of them as the last 10% after your foundations—sleep, nutrition, training, and stress—are in order. Used wisely, certain supplements can improve energy, fill nutrient gaps, and support your goals. Used poorly, they waste money and sometimes cause harm. This guide will teach you the essentials: evidence strength, safety, doses, and how to shop smart.

First, check the big rocks

Before you buy, audit your habits. Do you sleep 7–9 hours, eat protein and produce at most meals, and move daily? Supplements cannot fix chronic sleep deprivation or a consistently low‑protein diet. If those bases are covered, move on to smart additions below.

Supplements worth considering

  • Creatine monohydrate: Supports strength and cognition. Dose: 3–5 g/day. Safe for long‑term use for most healthy adults.
  • Vitamin D3: If levels are low and sunlight is limited. Dose varies; confirm with a blood test. Avoid megadoses without supervision.
  • Omega‑3 (EPA/DHA): Supports heart and brain health. 1–2 g combined EPA+DHA daily from fish oil or algae oil.
  • Magnesium glycinate: Helpful for muscle relaxation and sleep quality. Typical dose 200–400 mg elemental magnesium in the evening.
  • Protein powder: Convenience option, especially post‑workout or for breakfast. Aim for 20–40 g per serving, check for third‑party testing.

Supplements to approach cautiously

  • Fat burners and detoxes: Often underdosed, over‑stimulating, and expensive. Focus on habits.
  • Test boosters: Most have weak evidence and risk side effects. Strength training and sleep have larger effects.
  • Proprietary blends: Hide exact doses—hard to evaluate efficacy.

How to shop smart

  • Look for third‑party testing: NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Choice, or USP Verified.
  • Short ingredient lists: Fewer fillers and sweeteners are usually better.
  • Transparent dosing: Avoid proprietary blends that obscure amounts.
  • Reasonable prices: If it sounds too good to be true, it probably is.

Pros and cons

Pros
  • Can fill nutrient gaps and support performance
  • Convenient for busy schedules
  • Some have strong evidence and safety profiles
Cons
  • Industry is under‑regulated; quality varies
  • Can be expensive without clear benefit
  • Risk of interactions; consult your clinician

FAQs

Do I need a multivitamin? If your diet is varied and you have no deficiencies, not necessarily. A targeted approach based on labs is often better.

Is creatine safe? Yes for most healthy adults at recommended doses. Stay hydrated and discuss with your healthcare provider if you have kidney issues.

Which protein powder is best? Whey isolate for lactose‑tolerant individuals; whey concentrate for value; or a pea/rice blend for plant‑based eaters. Choose third‑party tested products.

Can supplements replace food? No. Food is the foundation for micronutrients, fiber, and satisfaction.
